# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best, “Two-Clique Degree-Profile Codebook” (Generation 10), establishes a new leading result at 16,945,300.52, with 50/50 accepted cases.** Its decisive pattern is not merely adding degree information: it restricts codewords to disjoint unions of up to two cliques plus isolates, creating degree sequences with a compact, highly structured form. Greedy maximin selection then explicitly separates templates in the same sorted-degree-profile space used by decoding.

- **Joint edge-count and degree-profile decoding is now empirically validated when paired with an aligned structural codebook.** The current program scores each message using both noise-adjusted total-edge error and centered sorted-degree-profile error. This substantially exceeds edge-count-oriented hybrids such as:
  - “Hybrid Graph GED and Density Classifier” (Generation 11): **15,914,237.04**
  - “Adaptive Graph GED and Edge-Count Decoder” (Generation 14): **13,716,283.38**
  - Earlier best “Adaptive Graph Codebook Decoder” (Generation 12): **13,895,152.26**
  
  The result changes the earlier conclusion that degree features were generally harmful: degree information is highly effective when codeword construction is deliberately optimized around degree-profile separation.

- **Adaptive graph sizing remains a successful common pattern, but the current best uses structure to keep sizes practical at low noise.** It estimates an edge-statistics-driven graph size from \(M\), \(\epsilon\), and the flip-channel signal \(1-2\epsilon\), then enforces a structural floor of approximately \(2\sqrt{M}+1\). This ensures enough two-clique degree templates while avoiding an unnecessarily large graph where structural coding can carry message information efficiently.

- **Metric-aligned greedy codebook construction consistently performs well.** Earlier successful GED programs selected templates by greedy maximin permutation-invariant GED; the current best applies the same principle in its actual invariant feature space—sorted degree sequences—with a small edge-count reward. This alignment is stronger than selecting graphs under one notion of distance and decoding under another.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Edge-count-only noisy decoding is reliable but no longer score-leading.** “Adaptive Graph Canonicalization and Edge-Count Codec” (Generation 15) reached **10,506,611.46**, and “Noise-Adaptive Graph Message Codec” (Generation 16) reached **13,538,725.78**, both passing all cases. Their decoding only uses the received edge total, discarding the degree-shape information that the Generation 10 two-clique construction exploits.

- **The previous conclusion that degree-profile decoding worsens results must be narrowed.** Earlier degree-based hybrids scored relatively poorly:
  - Generation 4: **12,354,500.80**
  - Generation 8: **12,306,930.96**
  
  However, Generation 10 reaches **16,945,300.52**, and Generation 11’s density/degree hybrid reaches **15,914,237.04**. The poor results of Generations 4 and 8 therefore cannot be attributed to degree statistics alone. The more supported explanation is that their graph templates, weighting, sizing, or feature/codebook alignment were weaker than the current two-clique maximin design.

- **Exact small-graph GED is robust but is no longer the strongest overall scoring mechanism.** The previous best Generation 12 used exact unlabeled six-vertex codebooks and score-objective-driven edge-count sizing for **13,895,152.26**. It remains a strong correctness-preserving hybrid, but the current structured degree-profile codec surpasses it by over three million points, indicating that a low-dimensional but noise-robust structural family can outperform exhaustive small-graph orbit coding.

- **Generic density-spaced graph families do not match deliberately shaped degree signatures.** Generation 9’s “Hybrid Graph Isomorphism Communication Codec,” with heterogeneous latent degree profiles and invariant matching, scored **12,912,727.60**. Although correct on all cases, its broader generated-graph approach did not reach the separation and decoding consistency of the constrained two-clique family.

## Implementation Insights

- **Generation 10’s candidate family is compact, deduplicated, and permutation-invariant by construction.** `make_candidates(N)` enumerates clique sizes \((a,b)\), treats size-one cliques as isolates, sorts degree sequences, and removes duplicate profiles with a `set<vector<int>>`. Thus each candidate is represented by the invariant actually observed after arbitrary vertex shuffling: its sorted degree sequence.

- **The encoder and decoder optimize compatible quantities.** Codebook selection initializes from the empty graph and greedily selects the candidate maximizing its minimum squared degree-sequence distance from already selected templates:
  ```cpp
  nearest[i] = min(nearest[i],
      degree_distance_sq(candidates[i].deg, candidates[best].deg));
  ```
  It additionally rewards distinct edge counts:
  ```cpp
  value = nearest[i] + 0.025 * candidates[i].edges;
  ```
  At decode time, those same degree profiles are compared after centering, while total edge count is separately modeled. This codebook/decoder consistency is a likely reason the degree feature succeeds here where earlier degree variants did not.

- **The current best correctly models bit-flip effects on total edges and centered degrees.** For a template with `c.edges`, it uses:
  ```cpp
  expectedEdges = eps * L + (1.0 - 2.0 * eps) * c.edges;
  ```
  This is the same statistically coherent flipped-edge expectation used by prior strong edge-count codecs. It also rescales centered degree deviations by the same signal factor, preserving the expected attenuation of graph structure under edge flips.

- **The centered degree profile avoids double-counting global density.** The decoder subtracts `observedMean` from received degrees and `templateMean` from template degrees before comparison. Consequently, edge total carries global-density evidence, while centered degrees capture residual shape evidence—such as the separate high-degree groups produced by two cliques. This is more principled than simply combining raw degree and edge errors, because raw degrees contain the total-edge signal already measured by `edgeCount`.

## Performance Analysis

- **Generation 10 is the clear new leader at 16,945,300.52.** It exceeds the strongest previously reported program, Generation 12 at **13,895,152.26**, by **3,050,148.26 points** (about **22.0%**). It also exceeds the next strongest program in the supplied summaries, Generation 11 at **15,914,237.04**, by **1,031,063.48 points**.

- **All major evaluated variants passed 50/50 cases, so score differences reflect coding efficiency rather than validity.** Generation 10, Generation 11, Generation 12, and all other summarized programs were accepted on every validation case. The advantage of Generation 10 therefore comes from its graph-size/codebook/statistical-decoder tradeoff, rather than a relaxed correctness threshold.

- **The strongest scores now correlate with preserving more than one invariant, provided the graph family makes that invariant discriminative.** Edge-count-focused methods cluster roughly from **10.51M** to **13.90M**. The current two-clique degree-profile method reaches **16.95M**, while Generation 11’s hybrid density/degree signature reaches **15.91M**. This supports a refined empirical pattern: sorted degree information can yield major gains over edge count alone when templates have intentionally separated degree shapes.

- **The score ordering also shows that exact GED is not required for top performance.** Small-graph GED methods were previously advantageous because they exactly handled permutation ambiguity in low-noise settings. Generation 10 instead uses a scalable invariant representation for graphs potentially larger than six vertices, avoiding factorial permutation work while retaining structural information. Its substantially higher score indicates that this scalable structured-codebook approach delivered a better evaluated reliability-versus-graph-size balance than the earlier exact-GED/edge-count hybrids.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Replace the fixed `0.025 * edges` greedy bonus with a decoder-matched candidate distance.** During codebook construction, score candidate separation using the same normalized feature geometry used at decode time: expected edge-mean separation divided by `edgeVar`, plus centered sorted-degree separation divided by `2*degreeVar`, with both multiplied by the flip-channel signal where appropriate. This should improve the already-successful metric alignment by selecting templates that are far apart under the actual noisy likelihood surrogate rather than under unscaled degree distance plus an ad hoc edge reward.

2. **Add swap-based local search after greedy two-clique selection.** Keep the current empty-seeded greedy maximin codebook, then repeatedly test replacing one selected candidate with an unselected two-clique candidate when it improves the minimum pairwise decoder-matched distance, or improves a lexicographic profile of all pairwise distances. Greedy maximin is a proven strong initializer, but the compact candidate family makes inexpensive \(O(M \cdot |\text{candidates}|)\) refinement practical and may remove poor tie-breaking choices.

3. **Optimize graph size \(N\) by evaluating the actual two-clique codebook and an estimated decoding error, not only the edge-statistics formula plus structural floor.** For a small range around the current proposed \(N\), build the candidate set and provisional greedy codebook, measure its minimum normalized edge/profile separation, and choose the smallest \(N\) whose estimated pairwise flip-noise confusion risk is sufficiently low. The present size rule is effective, but it does not account for the discrete number and spacing of available two-clique degree profiles, which are the central source of the current program’s advantage.

4. **Expand the structural family modestly beyond disjoint unions of two cliques while preserving compact invariant degree signatures.** A promising controlled extension is to include unions of up to three cliques, but only retain candidates whose sorted degree sequences remain distinct and whose edge counts/profile distances improve the selected codebook; alternatively add complements of the existing two-clique graphs. This directly builds on the successful deliberately shaped degree-profile principle while potentially providing additional well-separated templates for larger \(M\), without reverting to unstructured random graph families.

5. **Use a covariance-aware degree-profile score rather than treating centered sorted-degree coordinates as independent with one shared variance.** Precompute, for each template, the expected centered degree profile and approximate covariance induced by edge flips—degrees sharing an edge have positive covariance, and centering introduces dependence—then use a regularized diagonal-plus-low-rank Mahalanobis distance or weighted group-level statistic. The current centered-profile decoder is already a major success; better noise modeling can sharpen discrimination between two-clique templates whose high-degree blocks differ in clique size but have similar total edge counts.
