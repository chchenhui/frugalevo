# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best is structured destination lookahead, not merely greedy placement.** Generation 37, **Lookahead Stack Relocation Heuristic**, scored **9157.56** with **50/50 accepted**. For every possible non-source destination, it copies the exact post-move state, removes the current target, and simulates the next **32 target removals**. This improves substantially on the simple “latest-needed stack” greedy policy used by Generations 31, 34, and 35 (**9045.68**).

- **Using future relocation work as the primary criterion is effective.** The current program ranks candidate destinations by `predicted_energy`, adding `moved + 1` whenever a future target is buried in the rollout, then uses total moved boxes as a second criterion. This directly estimates the forced unblocking work induced by the current placement rather than relying only on a static stack statistic such as minimum label.

- **The rollout policy combines future-awareness with a strong greedy baseline.** During its 32-target simulations, Generation 37 selects each simulated relocation destination by the largest minimum contained label, then shorter height. This is the same core idea behind the strong **9045.68** greedy solvers, but used as a continuation policy inside lookahead. The result shows that the greedy rule is valuable as a rollout model even though it is weaker as the final decision rule.

- **Whole-suffix transfers and ascending extraction remain the universal accepted foundation.** Every strong solver—including Generation 37, the priority rollout solver (Generation 36, **9119.12**), and latest-minimum greedy solvers (**9045.68**)—removes targets in label order and transfers all blockers above a target as one contiguous suffix. This preserves legal move semantics and achieved acceptance on all 50 cases across the evaluated variants.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Fixed cyclic relocation is decisively weaker than state-dependent destination choice.** The sequential cyclic simulation scored only **8064.82**, despite also passing all 50 cases. It trails the current best by **1092.74 points**. The major loss comes from always choosing the next stack without considering height, low-priority box placement, or future obstruction.

- **Static stack summaries are useful but insufficient compared with replaying future behavior.** The latest-minimum greedy approach in Generations 31, 34, and 35 scored **9045.68**, which is strong but still **111.88 points below** Generation 37. Selecting the stack with the latest minimum label avoids immediately burying near-future targets, but it cannot distinguish destinations with similar minima and very different upcoming suffix-transfer costs.

- **More heuristic diversity or pseudo-random policy variation did not match targeted deterministic rollout.** The multi-policy planner (Generation 32) scored **9090.50**, and the diversified rollout planner (Generation 38) scored **9090.66**. Both were accepted, but they remain about **67 points below** the current best. Their broad policy sweeps, height penalties, top-label bonuses, and hash noise did not outperform Generation 37’s direct candidate-by-candidate simulation of future forced moves.

- **A longer rollout alone is not enough when the continuation policy is weaker.** Generation 39 used a **45-target** rollout but relied on shortest-stack moves during simulated continuation and scored **9038.94**, **118.62 points below** Generation 37. This indicates that the quality of the rollout policy and scoring objective matters more than simply extending the horizon.

## Implementation Insights

- **Generation 37 evaluates candidates from the true post-decision state.** For each candidate destination, it copies `stacks`, appends the current blocking suffix to that candidate, resizes the source to expose the target, and pops the target before beginning the future simulation. This avoids evaluating hypothetical states that do not correspond to the chosen move sequence.

- **Its lexicographic evaluation is aligned with the objective of avoiding future interference.** Candidate comparisons prioritize:  
  1. lower `predicted_energy`,  
  2. fewer relocated boxes,  
  3. lower eventual maximum stack height, and  
  4. larger minimum label among boxes that remain buried.  
  The first two criteria optimize predicted forced work directly; the final two provide deterministic balancing and urgency-aware tie-breaking when future costs are equal.

- **The code retains simple, reliable vector-based stack mutation.** Stacks are bottom-to-top `vector<int>` objects. A suffix move is encoded by outputting `stacks[source][pos + 1]` with the one-based destination, then implemented with `insert` and `resize`; target removal is emitted as `(target, 0)` and implemented with `pop_back`. This compact representation was validated by all accepted programs and avoids unnecessary data-structure complexity.

- **The fixed official stack count keeps all-destination simulation practical.** Unlike the earlier previous-best approach that pruned destination candidates to near-minimum-height stacks, Generation 37 evaluates **every non-source stack**. With the fixed small `m = 10`, this broader search remained accepted on all 50 cases and improved the prior reported **8868.08** best by **289.48 points**. The unused `stack_minimum` lambda has no performance role; the actual strength comes from the explicit per-candidate trial simulation.

## Performance Analysis

- **Generation 37 establishes a clear new best score of 9157.56.** It exceeds the previous reported lookahead best of **8868.08** by **289.48 points**, the priority-aware 68-target rollout solver (Generation 36, **9119.12**) by **38.44 points**, and the diversified policy solvers near **9090.5–9090.66** by roughly **67 points**. Since all these programs passed all 50 cases, the differences reflect solution quality rather than correctness or robustness.

- **The progression confirms that destination selection is the dominant optimization lever.** Scores rise from **8064.82** for fixed cyclic placement, to **8793.64** for shortest-stack placement with a top-label tie-break (Generation 33), to **9045.68** for largest-minimum-label greedy placement, and finally to **9157.56** for explicit lookahead. The largest gain is still moving away from fixed destinations, but future simulation provides the strongest final refinement.

- **Lookahead quality depends on both horizon and simulated policy.** Generation 36’s 68-target priority-aware rollout scored **9119.12**, while Generation 39’s 45-target shortest-stack rollout scored **9038.94**. Generation 37’s shorter **32-target** horizon nonetheless wins because it evaluates predicted relocation energy using a stronger latest-minimum continuation rule and additional tie-breaks for stack height and buried urgency.

- **All high-performing methods preserve the same legal operation structure, isolating the source of score gains.** Generation 37, Generations 31/34/35, Generation 36, and Generation 39 all use ascending target removal and contiguous suffix relocation. Therefore, the **111.88-point** advantage of Generation 37 over the best pure greedy score is specifically attributable to its candidate-specific future replay and ranking criteria, rather than to a change in move legality or extraction order.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Tune the candidate-specific rollout horizon around the proven 32-step baseline, including an adaptive variant.** Benchmark fixed horizons such as 24, 40, and 48 while retaining the same latest-minimum continuation policy and `predicted_energy` primary objective; Generation 39 shows that horizon alone is insufficient, so do not replace the rollout policy. Also test `H = min(48, 16 + 2 * moved)` where `moved` is the current suffix length, giving expensive immediate decisions more lookahead without paying the same simulation cost for trivial moves.

2. **Strengthen the rollout score with a discounted post-horizon obstruction estimate.** Keep `predicted_energy` and `predicted_boxes` lexicographically first, then add a state score at the end of the 32-step replay: for every remaining stack, measure how many boxes lie above each of the next 16–32 unremoved labels, weighted more heavily for nearer labels. This directly extends the best program’s “future forced work” concept to distinguish candidates whose costs occur just beyond the explicit rollout boundary.

3. **Use a stronger deterministic continuation policy during trial replay, but only for important simulated moves.** The current rollout uses largest stack minimum and shortest height, which is validated, but it may mis-rank candidates when future destination ties create different structures. For the first 4–8 simulated blocked targets, evaluate destinations using a cheap one-step score—immediate moved suffix size plus an urgency penalty for burying labels in `[future+1, future+12]`—then revert to the current latest-minimum policy for the rest of the horizon.

4. **Add a direct “collision of imminent labels” tie-break when evaluating the current destination.** For each candidate placement, inspect the moved suffix and candidate stack before simulation, and compute a penalty when placing a moved label in the next 16–32 targets above a candidate-stack label that is also imminent, especially when the candidate-stack label becomes deeply buried. Apply this after `predicted_energy`/`predicted_boxes` but before generic maximum-height balancing; it targets precisely the interference that static minimum-label summaries miss.

5. **Evaluate a small set of continuation-policy variants per candidate and score the candidate by robust or best predicted energy.** Generation 36’s priority-aware rollout was competitive and Generation 37 demonstrates that candidate-specific replay is the key advantage, so retain exact post-move trial states but simulate each candidate with two continuation policies: current latest-minimum/shortest-height and a priority-aware policy that favors destinations whose low labels are farther in the future. Choose by the lower predicted relocation work, or use the worse of the two as a robustness score, to reduce dependence on one rollout-policy bias without broad pseudo-random diversification.
