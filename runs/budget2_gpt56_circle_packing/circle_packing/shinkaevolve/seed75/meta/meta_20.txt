# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best, Generation 16 (`hexagonal_multistart_polish`), establishes the strongest demonstrated combination of search diversity and selective refinement.** It reached **sum_radii 2.63**, **validity 1.00**, and a perfect **combined score 1.00** in **3.65 s**. Its important improvement over the earlier 2.62 cluster was not a different core formulation, but a portfolio of asymmetric five- and six-row staggered seeds, fixed-center LP recertification, and a second SLSQP pass applied only to the strongest candidate contact graphs.

- **Joint nonlinear optimization of all centers and unequal radii remains the decisive quality transition.** Generation 16 optimizes all **78 variables**—52 center coordinates plus 26 independent radii—with wall and pairwise constraints. This is consistent with Generation 9 (`staggered_multistart`), which also earned **1.00** at **2.62**, and with Generations 10, 12, 13, 18, and 19, which all reached approximately **2.62** with perfect validity. The result confirms the prior insight that unequal radii must participate directly in the nonlinear search rather than being allocated only after a fixed placement is chosen.

- **Asymmetric staggered layered seeds reliably outperform single regular layouts.** Generation 16’s layouts include several placements of a six-circle row among five-circle rows, then additional six-row alternatives with four- and five-circle layers. Its deterministic per-seed perturbations alter boundary and interior contact graphs while retaining a dense triangular-lattice character. This extends the successful pattern from Generation 9’s competing layered layouts and Generation 10’s 15 unequal-row seeds, both of which achieved near-target quality.

- **Post-optimization LP radius recovery plus strict safety scaling is a robust high-scoring pattern.** Generation 16 recomputes the optimal radii for each fixed center arrangement through a linear program, then applies safety margins and pairwise roundoff correction. This preserved **validity 1.00** while allowing aggressive SLSQP optimization. Similar LP-based certification in Generation 19 reached **2.62 / 0.99**, while Generation 9 reached **2.62 / 1.00**; Generation 16 shows that this certification approach can support a measurable objective improvement when paired with stronger contact-graph exploration and selective polishing.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **More solver/certification machinery can fail entirely if it creates runtime, interface, or validation fragility.** Generation 15 (`contact_graph_portfolio`) used 14 perturbed seeds, two SLSQP stages, dense exact Jacobians, LP certification, and scaling, but received **zero** for score, sum, validity, and evaluation time. The evaluator classified it as incorrect despite its apparently similar geometry logic. This contrasts with Generation 16’s successful LP-plus-SLSQP implementation and shows that complexity alone was not correlated with successful evaluation.

- **Limited seed diversity can substantially reduce packing quality even when feasibility is perfect.** Generation 17 (`multistart_nonlinear_packing`) used only eight perturbed near-hexagonal starts and LP radii recomputation, but achieved only **sum_radii 2.40**, **target ratio 0.91**, and **combined score 0.91**. The evaluation feedback directly attributes this gap to its layered initialization and restricted local-search portfolio, compared with Generation 16’s much broader collection of row structures.

- **Conservative feasibility scaling can modestly reduce an otherwise strong objective.** Generation 14 (`diverse_hexagonal_multistart`) was valid but reached **2.61** in **4.40 s**, below both the 2.62 variants and Generation 16’s 2.63. Its feedback specifically notes that common-radius safety scaling may slightly reduce the optimized sum. Scaling is valuable for validity, but the evaluated results show that its exact interaction with the optimizer and final active constraints affects the final reported objective.

- **Runtime-heavy multistart designs did not automatically produce better results before the current best.** Generation 14 took **4.40 s** for **2.61**, and Generation 17 took **3.31 s** for **2.40**, whereas Generation 18 reached **2.62** in **1.41 s** and Generation 12 reached **2.62** in **1.54 s**. Generation 16 is an exception: its extra **3.65 s** corresponds to the only evaluated increase to **2.63**, because it directs the additional work toward polishing the best seven candidates rather than treating every restart equally.

## Implementation Insights

- **Generation 16’s constraint implementation is computationally appropriate for dense all-pairs packing.** It precomputes the **325** upper-triangular circle pairs using `np.triu_indices(26, 1)`, evaluates all squared-distance separation constraints vectorially, and supplies an explicit analytic Jacobian. Its constraint form,
  `||c_i - c_j||² - (r_i + r_j)² >= 0`,
  avoids square roots during SLSQP’s dominant nonlinear constraint calculations. This follows the strongest prior pattern from analytic-Jacobian variants such as Generations 9, 12, 13, and 18.

- **The current best separates center optimization from exact fixed-center radius allocation.** `_optimize` runs SLSQP jointly over centers and radii, but returns radii from `_certify(candidate_centers)` rather than blindly trusting the solver radii. `compute_max_radii` then solves the fixed-center linear program maximizing `sum(r_i)` subject to wall bounds and `r_i + r_j <= distance(i,j)`. This gives a clean post-solver objective evaluation for each discovered center contact graph and is a concrete explanation for Generation 16’s robust **validity 1.00**.

- **Selective second-stage polishing is a notable efficiency-quality mechanism in the current best.** The program first evaluates all 18 layered seed configurations with up to 850 SLSQP iterations, sorts candidates by LP-certified radius sum, then spends a second 500-iteration optimization only on the top seven. Earlier programs either used fewer starts—Generation 17’s eight starts—or broadly similar multistart approaches without this reported best-candidate polish and remained at **2.40–2.62**. Generation 16’s **2.63** suggests that small gains arise from refining promising contact graphs, not just adding unconstrained random restarts.

- **Feasibility handling is deliberately redundant and explains the consistently perfect validation outcome.** The program clips centers to the unit square, bounds LP radii by wall clearance, applies `SAFETY = 1 - 5e-9`, and performs a final pairwise roundoff correction. This preserves containment and non-overlap even if SLSQP terminates at a tolerance-level violation. That implementation matches the previous cross-generation evidence: nearly every successful SLSQP program used final global scaling or equivalent conservative postprocessing and reported **validity 1.00**.

## Performance Analysis

- **Generation 16 is the new evaluated high-water mark.** It improved the observed radius sum from the prior recurring **2.62** plateau to **2.63**, while retaining a perfect **1.00 combined score**, **1.00 target ratio**, and **1.00 validity**. Generation 9 also scored **1.00**, but at **2.62** and **2.68 s**. Thus, the current best’s main measurable advance is a roughly **0.01** increase in sum of radii, at an additional roughly **0.97 s** evaluation cost.

- **Most successful recent implementations form a very narrow quality cluster around 2.62.** Generations 10, 12, 13, 18, and 19 all recorded **2.62**, with combined scores of **0.99**, despite different numbers of starts, seed templates, and whether LP recovery was included. Their runtimes span about **1.41–2.42 s**. The cluster supports the prior conclusion that SLSQP with staggered layered initialization is reliably strong, but that surpassing 2.62 requires better center/contact-graph refinement rather than merely changing safety handling.

- **The score is highly sensitive to the final reported radius sum despite uniformly perfect validity among successful programs.** Generations 10, 12, 13, 18, and 19 all had **validity 1.00** but a **0.99** target ratio/combined score at 2.62. Generation 16’s 2.63 crossed the evaluator’s target-quality threshold and received **1.00**. Therefore, among already valid methods, the important evaluated distinction is not feasibility but the small objective increase from the contact graph and final radii allocation.

- **Evaluation time has weak correlation with packing quality except where computation is targeted effectively.** Generation 18 obtained **2.62** in **1.41 s**, Generation 12 in **1.54 s**, and Generation 14 only **2.61** in **4.40 s**. Generation 16’s **3.65 s** is slower than the 2.62 methods, but unlike Generation 14, it converts that additional work into the best observed result through LP-certified ranking and polishing only the best seven seed outcomes. The failed Generation 15 further shows that computational heaviness is not inherently beneficial and can introduce correctness risk.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Expand the seed portfolio with controlled variants around the proven five-row contact graphs.** Retain the best `(5,6,5,5,5)` and related permutations, but add mirrored versions, vertically reversed row orders, and independently varied left/right row margins rather than only changing the stagger amplitude and random perturbation. This directly targets new boundary-contact graphs while preserving the asymmetric layered structures that enabled Generation 16 to exceed the 2.62 plateau.

2. **Rank first-stage candidates using multiple near-optimal radius allocations, not only the single LP sum.** After each initial SLSQP solve, keep the LP-certified radii but also calculate diagnostics such as the number of near-active wall/pair constraints, smallest positive slack, and the nonlinear solver’s pre-certification objective; choose polish candidates from both the top LP sums and structurally distinct active-set signatures. Since the gain to 2.63 came from selectively polishing promising contact graphs, this reduces the risk that several nearly identical candidates consume all seven refinement slots.

3. **Use a continuation-style second polish with progressively tighter feasibility treatment.** For top candidates, first optimize with the current squared-distance constraints and radii scaled slightly below the LP solution, then run a final high-precision SLSQP pass initialized from that result with `ftol` around `1e-12` and a larger targeted iteration cap such as 800–1200. The current two-stage approach is already validated; a tighter final pass may recover small radius improvements at contact configurations near the 2.635 level without paying that cost for all 18 starts.

4. **Introduce deterministic anisotropic seed perturbations that move whole rows and selected interior circles coherently.** In addition to independent per-circle noise, apply small row-wise horizontal shifts, row-dependent vertical compression/expansion, and alternating interior offsets before the first solve, while clipping to a safe interior range. Independent perturbations diversify starts, but coherent deformations are more likely to reach different triangular-lattice and wall-contact topologies—the mechanism most plausibly responsible for the narrow improvement beyond 2.62.

5. **Add a small local “center-only” LP-guided perturb-and-reoptimize phase for the best certified layouts.** For each selected candidate, identify circles with tight LP wall or pair constraints, perturb only a few non-boundary or weakly constrained centers by deterministic offsets of roughly `1e-3` to `5e-3`, recompute feasible LP radii, and launch short joint SLSQP solves from those variants. This extends the successful LP-certification-plus-polish pattern: the LP exposes which local contact graph features limit the radius sum, while short targeted escapes can discover a neighboring graph with a slightly better certified allocation.
