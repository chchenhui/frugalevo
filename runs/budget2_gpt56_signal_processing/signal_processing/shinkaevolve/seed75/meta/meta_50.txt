# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best multiscale robust causal trend filter achieves the strongest measured composite quality through conditional fast/slow consensus plus output-level reversal control.** Its **0.53 combined score and leading 0.49 composite score** accompany **103.2 slope changes, 84.8 false reversals, and 0.16 smoothness**. This is the best documented balance of stability and viable tracking, despite lower raw reconstruction metrics (**0.88 correlation, 0.72 responsiveness, 0.38 lag error**) than the more reactive Kalman and LOESS alternatives.

- **Conditional blending of two causal robust local-linear endpoint fits is consistently stronger for directional stability than a single rapid state tracker.** The current best uses fast and slow recency-weighted Huber fits, raises the fast-model weight only when slopes agree, and constrains that weight to **0.18–0.68**. Generation 44’s *Robust Multiscale Causal Trend Filter* independently supports this pattern: it achieved **0.53 combined**, **0.16 smoothness**, only **102 slope changes**, and **80.6 false reversals**, substantially below most Kalman-family reversal counts.

- **Persistence-based reversal publication gates improve stability more effectively than innovation clipping alone.** The current best attenuates sub-threshold steps to **22%**, reduces rejected opposing moves to **10%**, and accepts a reversal only after strong model support or accumulated two-step evidence. This aligns with its relatively low **84.8 false reversals**. Generation 47’s confirmed dual-scale alpha-beta tracker also benefited from confirmation logic, reaching **97.4 false reversals** and **0.14 smoothness**, better than the more permissive Kalman outputs but still behind the current best.

- **Robust estimation components preserve correctness while enabling conservative output behavior.** Huber reweighting, MAD-based difference scales, residual-scale floors, bounded innovations, and limited predictive lead appear repeatedly in successful programs. The current best maintains **1.00 success and efficiency scores** despite a more computationally involved implementation, while Generation 44 and Generation 45 also passed all validation checks using robust causal regression and robust scale estimation.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Highly reactive Kalman-style tracking with relatively permissive output hysteresis consistently leaves excessive directional jitter.** Generation 48’s *Adaptive Kalman Trend Filter with Reversal Gating* reached strong tracking metrics—**0.92 correlation, 0.76 responsiveness, 0.32 lag error, and 0.46 noise reduction**—but only **0.11 smoothness**, with **160.6 slope changes** and **125.4 false reversals**. Generation 49 showed the same pattern: **0.92 correlation** and **0.34 lag**, but **150.2 slope changes** and **120 false reversals**.

- **Short or weak reversal-confirmation policies do not adequately suppress noisy turns.** Generation 37’s two-sample directional persistence gate had the weakest stability among the listed robust-regression variants, with **229.4 slope changes**, **180.8 false reversals**, and only **0.08 smoothness**, despite **0.90 correlation** and **0.80 responsiveness**. Its adaptive fast-model weighting remained too responsive when local trends fluctuated.

- **Fast endpoint LOESS-style adaptation improves correlation and lag but can sacrifice the metrics driving composite quality.** Generation 45’s causal robust multiscale LOESS implementation achieved **0.91 correlation**, **0.76 responsiveness**, and **0.32 lag**, but retained **150 slope changes**, **118.8 false reversals**, and **0.12 smoothness**. The comparison with the current best shows that endpoint alignment by itself is not enough; the blending and reversal-publication policy determine whether local endpoint variation becomes visible directional jitter.

- **Sophisticated adaptive alpha-beta logic can fail entirely when tracker dynamics mismatch evaluation behavior.** Generation 46’s reliability-weighted causal adaptive tracker passed validation but scored **0.00 overall/composite**, with reported **0.00 correlation and noise reduction**, **1.00 lag and average error**, and **50 false reversals**. Its predictive lead, adaptive tracker behavior, and hysteresis mechanisms did not yield measurable quality under the evaluator, showing that complexity without stable output behavior can be much worse than the robust regression and Kalman families.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best’s core advantage is that it uses model agreement to control reactivity before applying output hysteresis.** It computes separate fast and slow endpoint levels/slopes, measures normalized slope disagreement using the robust noise scale, and reduces fast-model influence as disagreement rises. The resulting consensus level is therefore less reactive during uncertain local turns, which is consistent with its **103.2 slope changes** versus **150–191.6** for most Generation 43, 45, 48, and 49 adaptive filters.

- **Its noise scaling is deliberately resistant to both local spikes and unrealistically small estimates.** `noise_scale` combines a quick difference-MAD estimate, a slowly updated noise estimate, and minimum floors based on fast/slow fit residual scales. This is more robust than relying solely on recent innovations or first differences. The two-pass Huber local regression further limits isolated samples from shifting the endpoint estimate, supporting the current best’s low false-reversal result despite modest overall noise reduction (**0.29**).

- **The stability mechanism operates directly on displayed output steps rather than only on latent state estimates.** The code compares each candidate to the previous published estimate, establishes a displayed direction, dampens tiny steps, and tracks pending opposite-direction distance/count. This is closely matched to the evaluator’s slope-change and false-reversal measurements. By contrast, Generation 49 explicitly kept held outputs separate from the Kalman predictor, which preserved state tracking but still yielded **150.2 slope changes** and **120 false reversals** in the visible output.

- **The current best accepts slower execution because runtime is not presently a limiting score factor.** At **0.30 s**, it is materially slower than Generation 48’s **0.02 s** and the **0.03–0.05 s** Kalman implementations, due to repeated fast/slow weighted regressions, Huber reweighting, and robust scale calculation at every sample. # META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Replace binary slope agreement with a continuous, magnitude-aware consensus confidence.** Compute directional alignment as `alignment = (fast_slope * slow_slope) / (abs(fast_slope) * abs(slow_slope) + eps)` and combine it with normalized slope magnitudes, so near-zero or weakly aligned slopes do not receive the same treatment as strong agreement. Use this confidence continuously to adjust both `fast_weight` and `gain`; this preserves the successful dual-scale architecture while avoiding abrupt reactivity changes from the current Boolean `slope_agreement`.

2. **Tighten the immediate reversal bypass while retaining the existing two-sample fallback.** Change `model_confirmed` so the fast-and-slow-support branch also requires a minimum normalized consensus slope, such as `abs(consensus_slope) / noise_scale >= 0.35–0.50`, and require the fast-only sharp-turn branch to meet a higher threshold, such as `>= 0.90–1.10`. The current output gate is the major source of its stability advantage, so making immediate reversals depend on both sign agreement and directional strength is a promising route to reduce the remaining 84.8 false reversals without globally increasing lag.

3. **Add reliability-aware downweighting of the fast endpoint fit.** Before calculating `fast_weight`, derive a short-horizon reliability score from `fast_fit_scale / max(slow_fit_scale, eps)` and the newest fast-fit residual; reduce fast influence when the fast residual scale is much larger than the slow scale or when the latest observation is an outlier. For example, multiply the activity-derived fast contribution by `1 / (1 + max(0, fast_fit_scale / slow_fit_scale - 1))`; this extends the existing Huber robustness by preventing a noisy short fit from becoming influential after it has already been estimated.

4. **Introduce decaying and consistency-checked pending reversal evidence.** Keep `pending_count` and `pending_distance`, but decay pending evidence, e.g. `pending_distance *= 0.75–0.85`, whenever the candidate weakens, reverses back toward the established direction, or loses fast/consensus slope support. Require persistence samples to have at least partial directional support from `consensus_slope`, rather than accepting two merely opposite output steps; this should suppress alternating endpoint noise while preserving the current filter’s effective output-level hysteresis.

5. **Make predictive lead turn-aware instead of applying a fixed velocity fraction.** Scale the `0.32 * velocity` lead down when the fast slope decelerates relative to `velocity`, when fast and slow slopes disagree, or when `consensus_slope * velocity <= 0`; retain the full bounded lead only during coherent continuation. This selectively reduces overshoot before turns—a likely contributor to visible false reversals—while retaining causal compensation during stable trends, where the current model needs help improving its 0.38 lag error and 0.72 responsiveness.
