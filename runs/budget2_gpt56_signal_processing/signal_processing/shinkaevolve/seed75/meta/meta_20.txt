# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best, Adaptive Trend-Preserving Kalman Filter, confirms that a causal two-state level/slope model is the strongest approach.** Its full level-and-slope covariance propagation achieved the leading **0.53 combined score**, **0.94 correlation/accuracy**, **0.55 noise reduction**, and **0.26 lag/average error**. This improves materially on the moving-window baseline pattern described in prior results, which reached roughly **0.50 combined**, **0.84 correlation**, **0.33 noise reduction**, and **0.38 lag**.

- **Robust local measurement-noise estimation from first differences is associated with the best denoising results.** The current best initializes measurement variance from startup MAD of first differences and recomputes a local MAD over the latest trailing window, blending it through `0.9 * measurement_var + 0.1 * local_r`. Its **0.55 noise reduction** exceeds the newer summarized Kalman variants: Generations 11–21 generally produced **0.41–0.48 noise reduction** at **0.52 combined score**.

- **Innovation clipping before state correction and process-noise adaptation is a consistently productive robustness pattern.** The best program clips each raw residual to `±3 * sqrt(measurement_var + p00)` and uses the clipped innovation both for level/slope correction and for bounded process variance. This limits impulse-driven gain escalation while retaining **0.79 responsiveness**. The related robust Kalman programs in the earlier evaluation also reached **0.53**, supporting the prior finding that clipped, variance-aware updates outperform direct adaptive alpha-beta gain schedules.

- **Causal trailing-edge alignment preserves responsiveness without conventional moving-average delay.** The current best emits the state estimate only from `t >= window_size - 1`, maintaining the required output length and alignment while estimating the newest sample. This explains its **0.26 lag error**, substantially better than the alpha-beta implementations such as Generation 14 (**0.35 lag**) and Generation 15 (**0.37 lag**), while retaining stronger correlation and denoising.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **More aggressive short-scale endpoint regression/fusion increased directional instability without exceeding the best score.** Generation 13’s Robust Multi-Scale Endpoint Trend Filter reached only **0.51 combined** despite very high responsiveness (**0.85**) and low lag (**0.18**). Its adaptive fusion created **345.6 slope changes** and **275.8 false reversals**, the highest instability in the provided summaries, and yielded only **0.05 smoothness**.

- **Alpha-beta trackers remained fast but did not achieve the Kalman filter’s denoising or accuracy balance.** Generation 14 achieved **0.52 combined**, **0.90 correlation**, and **0.28 noise reduction**; Generation 15 similarly achieved **0.52**, **0.88 correlation**, and **0.29 noise reduction**. Although their reversal counts were lower than the current best’s—Generation 15 had **105.6 slope changes** and **81.8 false reversals**—their weaker noise reduction and higher lag constrained overall performance.

- **Stronger reversal gating and hysteresis did reduce direction-change counts, but did not raise the combined score.** Generations 17 and 19 used Joseph covariance updates and Schmitt-trigger-style reversal gates, reducing instability to **182.8 slope changes** and **143.8 false reversals**, versus the current best’s **277.8** and **219.2**. However, both remained at **0.52 combined**, with lower **0.91 correlation**, **0.42 noise reduction**, and higher **0.33 lag** than the current best.

- **Innovation-responsive slope adaptation can remain overly reactive even when clipping is present.** Generation 16 used robust first-difference noise estimation, clipped innovations, and bounded slope corrections, but produced **248.2 slope changes** and **193.2 false reversals**, with only **0.07 smoothness** and **0.52 combined**. This mirrors the current best’s main weakness: rapid adaptation improves tracking metrics but leaves substantial residual directional fluctuation.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best’s effectiveness comes from maintaining the full two-state covariance rather than directly scheduling alpha-beta gains.** It propagates `p00`, `p01`, and `p11`, calculates separate level and slope gains from predicted covariance, and updates uncertainty sequentially. This structured uncertainty model corresponds to its best-in-set **0.94 accuracy/correlation**, **0.55 noise reduction**, and **0.26 error**, outperforming the simpler alpha-beta variants’ **0.88–0.92 correlation** and **0.28–0.38 noise reduction**.

- **Its process-noise schedule is bounded and uses clipped residual energy.** The code computes `process_var` as a small measurement-variance baseline plus `0.01 * innovation^2`, capped at `0.25 * measurement_var`. This is an important implementation distinction: genuine changes can increase adaptation, but a clipped outlier cannot make process noise arbitrarily large. The resulting balance is reflected in high **0.79 responsiveness** together with the strongest **0.55 noise reduction**.

- **The local-noise update is both robust and gradual.** Rather than treating every residual as a direct measurement-noise estimate, the current program uses the MAD of recent first differences and slowly EWMA-updates measurement variance. This avoids severe gain shifts from a single noisy sample and is consistent with the better denoising results relative to the later Kalman variants, whose noise reduction plateaued around **0.41–0.48**.

- **The current best code does not contain an explicit output reversal gate or slope-direction hysteresis after the Kalman update.** Unlike Generations 11, 12, 17, 19, and 21, whose summaries explicitly describe directional hysteresis, deadbands, or Schmitt-trigger gates, this implementation outputs the updated `level` directly. Its high **277.8 slope changes** and **219.2 false reversals**, alongside only **0.07 smoothness**, are consistent with that implementation difference even though its core estimation metrics remain superior.

## Performance Analysis

- **The observed best-score plateau remains 0.53, and the current program is the strongest member of that tier on accuracy and denoising.** It has **0.94 correlation**, **0.55 noise reduction**, and **0.26 lag**, while retaining perfect efficiency/success at **0.04 s**. Earlier related Kalman variants also reached **0.53**, but the current one is identified in the previous insights as the best reported configuration for correlation and noise reduction.

- **Most newer alternatives cluster tightly at 0.51–0.52 combined score despite substantial architectural variation.** Generations 9 and 13 scored **0.51**; Generations 11, 12, and 14–21 largely scored **0.52**. This indicates that adding hysteresis, Joseph-form covariance updates, alpha-beta gain schedules, or multiscale regression changed metric tradeoffs but did not surpass the current filter’s aggregate balance.

- **Lower lag and higher responsiveness do not necessarily translate to a higher combined score when noise reduction declines or directional instability rises.** Generation 13 had better lag (**0.18**) and responsiveness (**0.85**) than the current best (**0.26**, **0.79**) but lower correlation (**0.91**), lower noise reduction (**0.42**), and extremely poor smoothness (**0.05**), ending at **0.51 combined**. Similarly, the alpha-beta filters were highly efficient but did not match the current Kalman model’s accuracy/noise combination.

- **Smoothness is the persistent weakness across all high-performing causal trend filters, including the leader.** The current best’s **0.07 smoothness** is low despite its leading aggregate score, and its **277.8 slope changes / 219.2 false reversals** are worse than the gated Kalman variants. Gated alternatives improved these stability counts—for example, Generation 21 reached **178.4 slope changes** and **140.6 false reversals**—but their lower correlation (**0.90**) and noise reduction (**0.41**) kept them at **0.52**, showing that the evaluation currently rewards the best program’s tracking and denoising balance more strongly than directional stability alone.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Retain the two-state level/slope Kalman model, but grid-search the bounded process-noise schedule near the current optimum.** Sweep the baseline acceleration term (`0.002 * measurement_var`), innovation-energy coefficient (`0.01 * innovation**2`), and cap (`0.25 * measurement_var`) over conservative ranges such as baseline `0.001–0.004`, coefficient `0.005–0.015`, and cap `0.20–0.40`. The current clipped, bounded adaptive process noise is central to the leader’s 0.94 correlation and 0.55 noise reduction, so narrow tuning is more promising than replacing the state model.

2. **Use a Joseph-form covariance update with explicit symmetric positive-semidefinite safeguards.** Compute the full predicted covariance matrix, apply `P = (I-KH) P_pred (I-KH)^T + K R K^T`, force the off-diagonal terms to agree, and floor diagonal variances to a small positive value. This preserves the successful full-covariance design while reducing numerical drift, potentially allowing modestly more responsive process-noise settings without destabilizing gains or increasing false reversals.

3. **Add a soft, variance-scaled limit to only the slope correction rather than applying hard output hysteresis.** Keep `level` corrected by the normal Kalman gain, but replace `slope += gain_slope * innovation` with a clipped or smoothly shrunk increment whose maximum is tied to local first-difference scale, for example `±c * sqrt(measurement_var)` with `c` searched around `0.1–0.5`. Earlier hard reversal gates lowered slope changes but lost correlation and noise reduction; slope-only robustification directly targets the leader’s 277.8 slope changes and 219.2 false reversals while preserving fast level tracking.

4. **Replace the fixed 3σ innovation clip with an adaptive normalized-innovation threshold.** Track an EWMA or short-window MAD of `raw_innovation / sqrt(predicted_p00 + measurement_var)`, then set the clipping limit dynamically within conservative bounds such as `2.5σ–4.0σ`. Innovation clipping before both the state update and process-noise adaptation is already a proven strength, and adapting it should better distinguish persistent real changes from isolated impulses across changing noise regimes.

5. **Introduce dual-timescale robust measurement-noise estimation while preserving the gradual update behavior.** Estimate a fast MAD variance from roughly 6–10 recent first differences and a slow MAD variance from the existing `window_size` span, then blend them—for example, use a bounded fast/slow mixture before the existing slow EWMA update. The current local first-difference MAD is associated with the best 0.55 noise reduction; a dual-scale version can react to genuine volatility shifts sooner without allowing single-window noise or impulses to sharply alter Kalman gains.
