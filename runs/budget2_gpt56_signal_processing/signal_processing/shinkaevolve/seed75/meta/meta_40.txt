# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best Multiscale Robust Causal Trend Filter is the strongest stability-oriented design in the evaluated set.** It reaches **0.53 combined / 0.49 composite**, with the best reported balance of directional stability: **103.2 slope changes, 84.8 false reversals, and 0.16 smoothness**. Its combination of fast and slow causal robust local-linear fits materially improves on most Kalman variants, which commonly retain **148–198 false reversals** and **180–232 slope changes** despite similar combined scores.

- **Fast/slow model consensus is more effective than a single reactive estimator.** The current program blends endpoint estimates according to normalized slope agreement, increasing fast-model weight when slopes align and shifting toward the slow model when they disagree. This retains usable tracking performance—**0.88 correlation, 0.72 responsiveness, 0.38 lag error**—while producing substantially fewer reversals than the more reactive LOESS and Kalman alternatives. For example, Generation 29 robust multiscale LOESS achieved better **0.93 correlation** and **0.79 responsiveness**, but had **232.4 slope changes** and **181.4 false reversals**.

- **Persistence-based output reversal gating remains the clearest successful stability mechanism.** The current best attenuates sub-threshold output steps, reduces unconfirmed opposing steps to 10%, and only accepts a reversal after either strong model-confirmed evidence or persistence with accumulated movement. This is consistent with its leading **84.8 false reversals**, compared with **89.2** for Generation 34 and **97.4** for Generation 35, both alpha-beta dual-scale confirmation filters with somewhat less conservative directional logic.

- **Robust scale estimation, Huber weighting, and bounded correction are reliable shared components.** The current best uses MAD-based fast/slow difference scales, a Huber-reweighted endpoint fit, clipped consensus innovations, gain bounds of **0.16–0.50**, and predictive lead limited to **±0.65 noise scale**. These mechanisms preserve correctness and causal responsiveness while resisting impulsive samples, reflected in its **1.00 success rate and efficiency score**.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Higher-reactivity local regression improves immediate tracking but degrades directional stability.** Generation 29’s trailing robust multi-scale LOESS filter achieved **0.93 accuracy/correlation**, **0.79 responsiveness**, and **0.27 lag error**, but its relatively permissive reversal gate yielded only **0.08 smoothness**, **232.4 slope changes**, and **181.4 false reversals**. The current best’s lower **0.88 correlation** and slower **0.72 responsiveness** are offset by far better reversal suppression.

- **Kalman-style adaptation with output-only hysteresis did not sufficiently prevent zig-zagging.** Generations 31, 32, 33, and 36 all used robust adaptive constant-velocity or two-state Kalman-style tracking, clipped innovations, and hysteresis/deadbands, yet had **140–175.8 false reversals** and **180–223.4 slope changes**, with smoothness only **0.08–0.10**. Their strong correlation values—typically **0.91–0.93**—did not translate into superior composite performance because their displayed output remained too directionally reactive.

- **Two-sample or relatively permissive confirmation rules were insufficient in alpha-beta trackers.** Generation 34 produced **112.8 slope changes / 89.2 false reversals**, Generation 35 had **123.4 / 97.4**, and Generation 39 had **109.4 / 85.6**. These are improvements over Kalman variants, but their smoothness stayed at only **0.14–0.15**, below the current best’s **0.16**, indicating that confirmation based mainly on short persistence or slope consensus still admits noisy local turns.

- **More aggressive adaptive gains and predictive compensation appear to trade denoising and stability for lag reduction.** The Kalman and alpha-beta families generally obtain lower lag errors of **0.27–0.34** and higher correlations around **0.91–0.93**, but do not exceed the current best’s stability profile. Their noise reduction is often modest as well—e.g., **0.34** in Generation 34 and **0.37** in Generation 40—showing that rapid state response does not inherently eliminate short-term directional noise.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best is effective because its fast/slow blend is conditional rather than fixed.** It computes independent recency-weighted Huber endpoint fits, normalizes their slope difference by a robust noise scale, and limits the fast-model blend weight to **0.18–0.68**. Fast tracking is therefore allowed only when local directional evidence is coherent; disagreement shifts the estimate toward the long-horizon model. This control structure explains why it has much fewer slope changes than the more directly reactive Generation 29 LOESS implementation.

- **Its noise estimate is deliberately multilevel and resistant to single outliers.** `noise_scale` takes a blend of fast and smoothed slow MAD-derived difference scales, while also enforcing floors based on fast and slow fit residual scales. This avoids allowing either a recent spike or an unrealistically small local MAD to dominate adaptation. The Huber reweighting pass inside `_endpoint_linear_fit` further reduces endpoint corruption from isolated observations.

- **The current best applies stability controls at the output-step level, where the reversal metrics are measured.** After forming a candidate estimate, it compares the candidate-to-output step with the prior displayed direction. Tiny movements are damped to **22%**, rejected opposite-direction movements are reduced to **10%**, and accepted reversals require strong model evidence or two persistent opposing steps with sufficient cumulative distance. This is more directly aligned with the measured **84.8 false reversals** than Kalman implementations that primarily stabilize state covariance or innovations.

- **The design accepts a computational cost because efficiency is already saturated.** The filter recomputes two weighted regressions, performs a Huber reweighting pass, and calculates rolling robust scales for each sample, resulting in **0.30 s** runtime. This is slower than the Kalman implementations at roughly **0.02–0.04 s**, but all programs still score **1.00 efficiency**, so the extra work is effectively converted into better smoothness and fewer false reversals rather than penalized runtime.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Replace the binary `slope_agreement` / `coherence` switch with continuous directional confidence.** Compute alignment as `fast_slope * slow_slope / (abs(fast_slope) * abs(slow_slope) + eps)`, map positive alignment to `[0, 1]`, and use it smoothly in both `fast_weight` and `gain` rather than adding a fixed `0.16` fast-weight bonus and `0.10` gain bonus. This retains the successful fast/slow consensus structure while reducing abrupt behavior when one local slope is near zero, which may improve the current 0.38 lag without sacrificing its 84.8 false-reversal advantage.

2. **Strengthen reversal acceptance with a graded model-confirmation score rather than the current permissive two-condition bypass.** Require the strong-turn bypass to combine normalized consensus slope, fast/slow sign agreement, and endpoint separation—for example, permit immediate reversal only when both slopes support the turn and `abs(consensus_slope) / noise_scale >= 0.45`, or when fast support is exceptionally strong with `>= 1.0`. Keep the existing two-step persistence fallback, but test accumulated-distance thresholds around `0.60–0.85 * noise_scale`; this directly extends the output-level gate responsible for the leading 103.2 slope changes and 84.8 false reversals.

3. **Make the fast-model blend depend on fit reliability as well as slope activity.** Derive a fast reliability penalty from `fast_fit_scale / slow_fit_scale` and from the latest endpoint residual, then reduce `fast_weight` when the short fit is noisy or the newest point is impulse-like—for example, multiply the activity contribution by `1 / (1 + max(0, fast_fit_scale / slow_fit_scale - 1))`. The existing Huber pass limits corruption within each fit, but explicitly preventing a contaminated short horizon from dominating the consensus should improve noise reduction beyond 0.29 while preserving causal responsiveness.

4. **Use asymmetric persistence for reversals versus trend continuation.** Preserve immediate acceptance for movements in the established direction, but require 2–3 supporting samples for an opposing move unless model confidence is high; additionally, decay `pending_distance` slightly, such as `pending_distance *= 0.80`, whenever the candidate weakens or its slope support disappears. This mutation is closely aligned with the evaluation’s directional metrics and should reject intermittent alternating evidence more effectively than simple two-sample confirmation, addressing the remaining false reversals without broadly slowing valid continuation.

5. **Add a bounded “turn anticipation” term based on fast-slope deceleration before the output direction changes.** Detect when the fast slope is shrinking toward zero or opposing the stored `velocity`, then reduce predictive lead and correction gain before a reversal candidate appears; for example, scale the `0.32 * velocity` lead by a factor based on `max(0, fast_slope * velocity) / (abs(fast_slope * velocity) + noise_scale**2)`. Since predictive compensation improves tracking but can contribute to overshoot around turns, selectively suppressing it during slope disagreement may lower reversal counts further while avoiding the poor lag and responsiveness associated with globally reducing gain or lead.
