# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **Causal level-and-slope state estimation is the strongest recurring pattern.** The current best program, **Adaptive Trend-Preserving Kalman Filter**, reached a **0.53 combined score**, improving over the original exponentially weighted moving-window filter’s **0.50**. Its two-state level/slope model achieved **0.94 correlation/accuracy**, **0.26 lag error**, **0.55 noise reduction**, and **0.79 responsiveness**, showing that explicit trend prediction reduces trailing-moving-average delay without losing denoising performance.

- **Robust noise estimation from first differences plus bounded innovation updates performs well.** The current best computes initial and local measurement variance using MAD-scaled first differences, EWMA-smooths that variance (`0.9 * old + 0.1 * local`), and clips innovations at `3 * sqrt(measurement_var + p00)`. This combination coincides with its strongest-in-set noise reduction (**0.55**) and accuracy (**0.94**). Similar robust Kalman variants in Generations 5 and 6 also achieved **0.53**, with noise reduction of **0.54**.

- **Bounded innovation-dependent process noise preserves both responsiveness and correlation.** In the current best, process variance scales with squared clipped innovation but is capped at `0.25 * measurement_var`. This produces adaptive response during changes while preventing unlimited covariance/gain growth. Its **0.79 responsiveness** is substantially above the basic exponential filter’s **0.73**, while its lag is lower (**0.26** versus **0.38**).

- **Endpoint/trailing-window output alignment is consistently effective.** The current program emits the causal state estimate beginning at `t >= window_size - 1`, preserving required output length and newest-sample alignment. This alignment strategy also supported strong results in Generation 5 (**0.23 lag**, **0.81 responsiveness**) and Generation 6 (**0.20 lag**, **0.83 responsiveness**), rather than introducing moving-average-style window-center delay.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Pure moving-average or exponentially weighted window smoothing was not competitive on the combined metric.** Generation 0’s exponentially increasing recency weights produced acceptable responsiveness (**0.73**) and correlation (**0.84**), but only a **0.50 combined score**, with weaker noise reduction (**0.33**) and higher lag (**0.38**) than the current Kalman implementation. Window averaging lacks an explicit slope state, so it cannot predict through evolving trends as effectively.

- **More elaborate endpoint regression increased cost without exceeding the best score.** Generation 3’s robust recency-weighted linear/quadratic regression with two-pass Huber IRLS achieved only **0.52**, below the current best’s **0.53**, despite good **0.93 correlation** and **0.44 noise reduction**. It also had the slowest reported runtime (**0.17 s** versus the current program’s **0.04 s**) and still showed weak smoothness (**0.09**) with **205.2 slope changes**.

- **Reversal hysteresis/confirmation reduced oscillation counts but did not improve the overall score.** Generation 7’s Schmitt-trigger reversal gate reduced false reversals to **143.8** and slope changes to **182.8**, materially better than the current best’s **219.2** and **277.8**. However, it scored **0.52**, with lower correlation (**0.91**), weaker noise reduction (**0.42**), and higher lag (**0.33**). Generation 10 similarly improved directional stability further (**129.8 false reversals**, **169.2 slope changes**) but remained at **0.52**.

- **Alpha-beta gain scheduling preserved speed but did not materially improve smoothing.** Generations 2 and 8 ran in **0.01 s** and retained good correlation (**0.92**) and responsiveness (**0.76–0.77**), but their combined scores stayed at **0.51**. Their noise reduction was only **0.38**, and they retained high false-reversal counts (**194.6** in Generation 2 and **158.4** in Generation 8), indicating that adaptive gain scheduling alone did not match the current program’s robust covariance-based denoising.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best’s Kalman covariance propagation is a productive implementation detail.** It maintains full two-state uncertainty terms (`p00`, `p01`, `p11`) and calculates separate level and slope gains from predicted covariance. This is more structured than the alpha-beta trackers’ directly scheduled gains and aligns with the current program’s leading **0.94 correlation**, **0.55 noise reduction**, and low **0.26 average error**.

- **Robust variance is updated locally but gradually.** For each sample, the current implementation calculates the MAD of differences in the latest `window_size` region, then updates measurement variance with a slow EWMA. This avoids abrupt gain changes from a single contaminated local window, consistent with the feedback that robust local noise estimation and innovation clipping supported strong accuracy and responsiveness.

- **Innovation clipping is applied before both state and process-noise adaptation.** The current program clips `raw_innovation` before using it to update the state and derive `process_var`. That ordering limits the influence of impulsive samples on both immediate correction and subsequent filter reactivity. The robust Kalman family consistently outperformed the initial weighted-window baseline in correlation, lag, and noise reduction.

- **The implementation remains computationally lightweight despite adaptivity.** The current best uses a single sequential loop, short local difference windows, scalar covariance updates, and no iterative fitting. It retained an **efficiency score of 1.00** at **0.04 s**. By contrast, Generation 3’s repeated weighted regression and two-pass IRLS raised execution time to **0.17 s** without a combined-score advantage.

## Performance Analysis

- **The best observed combined score plateau is 0.53, reached by the current program and two related Kalman variants.** The current best achieved **0.53 / 0.43 composite**, matched by Generation 5 and Generation 6. Within that tie, the current program has the best reported **correlation/accuracy (0.94)** and **noise reduction (0.55)**, while Generation 6 has better lag/responsiveness (**0.20 lag**, **0.83 responsiveness**) and Generation 5 is also faster (**0.02 s**).

- **Accuracy and denoising improved steadily from the original filter to adaptive Kalman designs.** Generation 0 had **0.84 correlation**, **0.33 noise reduction**, and **0.38 lag**. The current best improved these to **0.94**, **0.55**, and **0.26**, respectively. This is the clearest measured benefit of replacing window-only smoothing with robust trend-state estimation.

- **High responsiveness is associated with the persistent smoothness weakness.** The current best has **0.79 responsiveness** but only **0.07 smoothness**, alongside **277.8 slope changes** and **219.2 false reversals**. Generation 6 pushed responsiveness still higher to **0.83** and lag lower to **0.20**, but its directional-instability counts became even higher (**309.8 slope changes**, **247 false reversals**) and smoothness declined to **0.06**.

- **Directional-stability mechanisms trade off against accuracy/noise metrics under this evaluation.** Generation 10 had the best reversal statistics among the summarized Kalman variants—**129.8 false reversals** and **169.2 slope changes**—and a higher smoothness score (**0.11**) than the current best. Yet it had lower correlation (**0.91**), weaker noise reduction (**0.43**), higher lag (**0.33**), and a lower combined score (**0.52**). The evaluation therefore favors the current program’s robust adaptive tracking balance over stronger reversal suppression.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Keep the two-state causal Kalman structure, but tune the acceleration-noise schedule around a lower baseline and a slightly higher change-response cap.** Grid-search the baseline coefficient (`0.002 * measurement_var`), innovation coefficient (`0.01 * innovation**2`), and cap (`0.25 * measurement_var`) over narrow ranges such as baseline `0.001–0.005`, innovation `0.005–0.02`, and cap `0.15–0.40`. The current bounded innovation-dependent process variance is a core reason for the best program’s 0.94 correlation, 0.55 noise reduction, and 0.79 responsiveness, so controlled parameter refinement is more promising than replacing the model.

2. **Replace the fixed 3σ innovation clip with an adaptive robust clip based on recent normalized innovations.** Maintain a short rolling or EWMA MAD estimate of `raw_innovation / sqrt(predicted_p00 + measurement_var)` and set the clip threshold to a bounded range, for example `2.5–4.0` robust standard deviations. The current ordering—clip before both state correction and process-noise adaptation—is demonstrably valuable; adapting its threshold could suppress excess slope changes and false reversals without losing the current filter’s ability to follow genuine transitions.

3. **Use a Joseph-form covariance update and explicitly enforce covariance symmetry/positive semidefiniteness.** After calculating the two gains, update the 2×2 covariance as `(I - K H) P_pred (I - K H)^T + K R K^T`, then set `p01` consistently from the symmetric off-diagonal and floor `p00`/`p11` to a small positive value. The existing full covariance propagation is a successful differentiator from alpha-beta variants, and a numerically robust covariance update may permit more aggressive but stable gain/process-noise tuning.

4. **Add a lightweight slope-specific robustification rather than a hard reversal gate.** Clip or shrink only the slope correction, e.g. `slope += clip(gain_slope * innovation, -max_slope_step, max_slope_step)`, where `max_slope_step` is tied to local difference scale, while leaving the level update largely unchanged. Prior reversal hysteresis reduced instability but harmed correlation and lag; limiting abrupt slope impulses should target the current weak smoothness/reversal metrics while retaining the high-quality causal level tracking.

5. **Blend robust local noise scales across two time horizons instead of relying on one `window_size` difference region.** Compute a fast MAD variance from a short recent span (such as 6–10 differences) and a slow MAD variance from the existing window, then use a bounded blend or EWMA—fast scale for temporary gain adaptation and slow scale for stable measurement variance. The current local MAD plus slow 0.9/0.1 EWMA is strongly associated with top noise reduction, while a dual-timescale version could react faster to changing noise regimes without allowing isolated spikes to dominate the Kalman gains.
