# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **Robust local endpoint trend estimation plus a causal tracker is the strongest observed pattern.** The current best, **Robust Adaptive Local-Trend Filter** (Generation 8), reached the highest combined score, **0.55**, by fitting an exponentially weighted local linear regression over each trailing window and evaluating it at the newest sample. This preserves endpoint alignment and avoids the substantial delay of a moving average. Its adaptive alpha-beta-style level/velocity update then smooths the regression measurement. This combination yielded **0.89 correlation**, **0.40 average error**, **0.39 lag error**, and only **102.6 slope changes / 81.2 false reversals**, substantially better directional stability than the Kalman trend variants.

- **MAD-based local outlier clipping materially improves stability.** Generation 8 computes a median and MAD independently per window, then clips values at median ± `3.5 * 1.4826 * MAD` before fitting. This robust preprocessing appears central to its advantage over more globally innovation-driven methods: the current best’s **81.2 false reversals** is far below Generation 7’s hybrid Kalman consensus (**199**) and Generations 4–6’s robust Kalman variants (**220–264**), while retaining a competitive **0.72 responsiveness**.

- **Exponential trailing-window weighting successfully reduces lag without abandoning smoothing.** The best program weights older samples down with `exp(linspace(-3, 0, window_size))`, allowing local regression to follow non-stationarity. Similar trend-preserving causal designs performed consistently well: Generation 7’s Kalman/consensus filter scored **0.54** with **0.94 correlation**, **0.61 noise reduction**, and **0.27 lag/average error**. However, Generation 8’s simpler robust local-trend approach produced much better smoothness behavior, reflected in its leading **0.16 smoothness score**.

- **Explicit reversal suppression is associated with the largest smoothness improvement.** The current best suppresses small level steps against established velocity when they fall inside a volatility-scaled reversal band. Relative to prior filters that remained highly reactive, this reduced slope changes from roughly **251–329** (Generations 6–7) to **102.6**, and false reversals from **199–264** to **81.2**. This is the clearest observed change associated with the score increase from **0.52–0.54** to **0.55**.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Over-reactive innovation-adaptive Kalman tuning can catastrophically destabilize filtering.** Generation 2’s **Adaptive Constant-Velocity Kalman Signal Filter** achieved a combined score of **0.00** despite perfect responsiveness/efficiency and zero lag. Its adaptive measurement/process variance behavior was evidently too aggressive: correlation collapsed to **0.03**, average error reached **63.55**, and it produced **422.4 slope changes** and **347.2 false reversals**. Avoiding delay alone did not translate to useful filtering quality.

- **Kalman-style trend tracking without sufficiently strong directional stabilization consistently remained noisy.** Generations 4, 5, and 6 all scored **0.52**, with good correlation (**0.92**) and low lag/average errors (**0.21–0.28**), but weak smoothness scores (**0.06–0.07**) and excessive reversals. Even Generation 6’s consecutive-evidence slope-reversal handling still had **328.8 slope changes** and **264 false reversals**, indicating that the stabilization was much less effective than the current best program’s direct volatility-scaled suppression of small opposing level moves.

- **Complex fusion did not outperform the simpler local robust trend pipeline on the composite objective.** Generation 9 combined a Kalman tracker, robust two-pass IRLS exponentially weighted regression, and disagreement-dependent fusion. It retained good tracking metrics—**0.93 correlation**, **0.81 responsiveness**, **0.23 lag/average error**, **0.57 noise reduction**—but scored only **0.53** and required **0.17 s**, versus the current best’s **0.55** at **0.01 s**. Its fusion still generated **297 slope changes** and **236.6 false reversals**, so added estimator complexity did not solve the dominant smoothness issue.

- **Basic exponentially weighted or alpha-beta tracking improved responsiveness but insufficiently rejected local fluctuations.** Generation 0’s weighted sliding filter and Generations 1 and 3’s alpha-beta trackers clustered around **0.49–0.50**. They achieved reasonable correlation (**0.83–0.90**) and responsiveness (**0.71–0.74**) but had **166.8–196.8 false reversals** and smoothness scores only **0.07–0.09**. Weighting or prediction alone was inadequate without robust local fitting and effective reversal control.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best cleanly separates robust measurement formation from recursive temporal smoothing.** It first constructs `measurements` and `local_slopes` from clipped windowed data using a fixed weighted least-squares matrix, then applies a one-pass recursive tracker. This architecture is computationally efficient—**0.01 s**, efficiency **1.00**—while providing both local trend information and temporal continuity. The vectorized `sliding_window_view`, matrix solve performed once per call, and matrix multiplications for all window fits avoid expensive Python loops except for the unavoidable sequential tracker update.

- **Endpoint local-linear regression is more appropriate than trailing means for a low-lag objective.** In the current best, the design time axis ends at zero, so the regression intercept is directly the fitted estimate at the newest observation. This is a causal endpoint estimate rather than an average located in the past. It explains why the program maintains **0.72 responsiveness** and avoids the lag characteristics of the basic moving-average baseline, while still delivering better smoothness than the other causal predictive filters.

- **The gain schedule uses local trend evidence rather than a single fixed gain.** The current best defines `slope_evidence = abs(local_slope) / (abs(local_slope) + local_noise)` and raises alpha from **0.42** toward **0.72** as local motion becomes stronger relative to noise. This provides a concrete mechanism for its balanced behavior: it does not treat all deviations as signal, yet allows faster response during coherent movement. That balance is consistent with its stronger smoothness result than Generations 1 and 3, whose adaptive alpha-beta trackers remained excessively reactive.

- **Its reversal test is tied to both volatility and current momentum.** The band `0.16 * local_noise + 0.08 * abs(velocity)` rejects only small steps that conflict with the existing direction. This preserves legitimate reversals better than a hard threshold while blocking low-amplitude sign flips. The observed reduction to **81.2 false reversals** is the best result among all evaluated enhanced approaches and is the implementation feature most aligned with the best **0.16 smoothness score**.

## Performance Analysis

- **Generation 8 is the best overall trade-off, not the best on every individual metric.** Its **0.55 combined score** exceeds Generation 7’s **0.54**, Generation 9’s **0.53**, and the robust Kalman group’s **0.52**. Generation 7 has stronger correlation (**0.94 vs. 0.89**), noise reduction (**0.61 vs. 0.39**), and lower lag (**0.27 vs. 0.39**), but its **251.2 slope changes** and **199 false reversals** are roughly 2–2.5 times the current best’s values. The evaluation therefore rewards Generation 8’s substantially stronger directional stability enough to offset its weaker raw denoising and tracking metrics.

- **Smoothness and reversal metrics are the primary differentiators among otherwise accurate causal filters.** Generations 4–7 and 9 generally achieved high correlation, from **0.92 to 0.94**, and low lag, from **0.21 to 0.28**, yet their combined scores plateaued at **0.52–0.54** because smoothness stayed at **0.06–0.07** and false reversals remained near or above **199**. The current best’s correlation is slightly lower, but its smoothness is more than double the typical Kalman-based score (**0.16 vs. 0.06–0.07**) and its reversal count is dramatically lower.

- **Responsiveness alone is not predictive of overall score.** Generation 2 had perfect responsiveness and zero lag but failed overall due to instability. Conversely, the best program’s responsiveness is a moderate **0.72**, below Generation 9 (**0.81**) and Generation 4 (**0.83**), but it produces the best composite outcome because its trend response is controlled rather than maximally aggressive.

- **Runtime is effectively saturated for all practical candidates, except the most complex fusion pipeline.** Generations 0, 1, 3, 7, and 8 ran in **0.01 s**, and the Kalman variants generally in **0.02–0.05 s**, all receiving efficiency **1.00**. Generation 9’s **0.17 s** is still validated and efficient by the scoring system, but it provides no overall-score advantage over the current best. Thus, the observed performance separation is driven by signal-quality metrics—especially false reversals and slope changes—rather than execution time.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Tune the reversal-suppression band with asymmetric, evidence-gated release logic.** Retain the successful volatility-scaled test, but require two consecutive opposing local-slope indications before allowing a suppressed reversal to pass; alternatively, reduce the band only when `local_slopes[i]` consistently opposes `velocity`. This directly extends the feature most associated with the best smoothness result, while avoiding permanent “sticking” when a genuine trend reversal begins.

2. **Replace fixed exponential weighting with a small adaptive family of causal weight decays.** Compute endpoint regressions using two or three precomputed decay rates (for example `exp(linspace(-1.5,0,W))`, `exp(linspace(-3,0,W))`, and `exp(linspace(-5,0,W))`) and select or blend them based on local slope evidence and residual/MAD noise. Use stronger recency weighting during coherent high-slope movement and gentler weighting in flat noisy regimes; this should improve responsiveness beyond 0.72 without discarding the robust endpoint-fit structure.

3. **Use regression residual scale, not only raw-window MAD, to drive clipping and gains.** After the initial clipped weighted fit, estimate a robust residual MAD around the fitted local line and use that residual scale for `slope_evidence`, reversal-band sizing, and optionally one additional Huber-style reweighting pass. This preserves the current program’s locally robust design while distinguishing true sloped signals from apparent window variability caused by legitimate trend movement.

4. **Add a bounded velocity-consensus update between the local slope and tracker velocity.** Instead of updating velocity solely from `step`, form a target velocity from a robust blend such as `0.5 * local_slope + 0.5 * step`, then clip its change relative to the previous velocity using a multiple of local residual noise. Local regression slope provides direct trend evidence, while bounded velocity acceleration can reduce lag on persistent motion without reproducing the unstable high-reversal behavior of reactive Kalman variants.

5. **Introduce a trend/flat-state hysteresis controller for alpha and beta schedules.** Maintain a lightweight state indicating whether the series is in a confirmed trend, using slope-to-noise evidence with separate enter and exit thresholds; in flat state use lower alpha and stronger reversal suppression, while in confirmed trend state use higher alpha and a moderately faster velocity update. The current continuous gain schedule is already effective, but hysteresis should prevent rapid gain switching around noisy thresholds and may further lower slope changes and false reversals.
