# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best Robust Adaptive Local-Trend Filter remains the strongest overall balance at a 0.55 combined score.** Its robust trailing-window pipeline—median/MAD clipping, exponentially weighted endpoint linear regression, then a causal alpha-beta tracker—produced the best directional stability: **102.6 slope changes**, **81.2 false reversals**, and **0.16 smoothness**. Its correlation (**0.89**) and responsiveness (**0.72**) are lower than the most aggressive Kalman variants, but its substantially lower directional jitter yields the highest composite result.

- **Robust local measurement construction before temporal tracking is repeatedly more effective than tracker-only innovation handling.** The current best clips every window using local median ± `3.5 * 1.4826 * MAD` before estimating level and slope. This contrasts with Generation 19’s adaptive Kalman innovation clipping (**206.2 false reversals**, **258.4 slope changes**, smoothness **0.07**) and Generation 29’s Huber/Kalman-regression fusion (**194.8/247.8**, smoothness **0.07**). Conditioning the local data before it enters a state update is associated with much more stable output direction.

- **Endpoint-aligned, recency-weighted regression continues to preserve causal trend response without the instability of highly predictive trackers.** The current best estimates the fitted level at the newest sample through a time coordinate ending at zero and exponential weights from `exp(-3)` to `1`. Newer endpoint-regression and consensus designs confirm the value of this causal alignment: Generation 23 and Generation 25 each achieved **0.54 combined**, **0.93 correlation**, and **0.29 lag error**, while retaining very fast **0.02 s** execution. However, their consensus trackers were less stable than the current best.

- **Direct countertrend-step suppression is more effective than short reversal confirmation alone.** The current best freezes a proposed movement when it opposes current velocity and is below a volatility- and velocity-scaled band, achieving **81.2 false reversals**. By comparison, the newer two-sample or persistence-based hysteresis designs remained materially noisier: Generation 20 had **151 false reversals**, Generation 23 had **123**, Generation 25 had **128.2**, and Generation 27 had **131.8**. The best program’s decision acts before a weak opposing step changes the reported output.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Reactive constant-velocity Kalman behavior remained the dominant source of poor smoothness.** Generation 19’s adaptive Kalman/hysteresis filter achieved good correlation (**0.91**) and lag (**0.29**), but its rapid state response yielded **258.4 slope changes**, **206.2 false reversals**, and only **0.07 smoothness**. Generation 22’s Kalman plus robust regression fusion was similarly responsive (**0.80**) and accurate (**0.93**) but reached **247 slope changes** and **193.8 false reversals**.

- **Multi-estimator consensus and fusion improved raw tracking metrics but not the composite score beyond the current best.** Generation 23’s Kalman-consensus filter and Generation 25’s alpha-beta consensus design both reached **0.54**, with **0.93 correlation** and roughly **0.56–0.57 noise reduction**. Yet they retained only **0.11 smoothness** and approximately **156–163 slope changes**, versus the current best’s **0.16 smoothness** and **102.6 slope changes**. Their predictive blending still passed too much local directional variation into the final output.

- **Short persistence, trend/flat modes, and hysteretic release mechanisms alone did not eliminate output jitter.** Generation 20’s two-sample directional hysteresis had **189.4 slope changes**; Generation 24’s stateful trend/flat controller had **159.2**; Generations 27 and 28 had approximately **161–163**. These controls reduced some isolated flips relative to the reactive Kalman variants, but their outputs remained substantially less stable than the current best’s direct weak-countertrend suppression.

- **Increasing regression sophistication through IRLS and multiscale profiles did not yield a stability advantage.** Generation 22’s two-pass IRLS fusion and Generation 29’s multi-profile endpoint blend both had strong **0.53** combined scores, **0.93 correlation**, and **0.80 responsiveness**, but their smoothness was only **0.07**. The added robust regression estimators improved local fit quality and low-lag tracking, but adaptive fusion introduced enough short-term slope disagreement to maintain very high reversal counts.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best efficiently separates vectorized local estimation from sequential temporal stabilization.** `sliding_window_view` forms every trailing window, then median, MAD, clipping, and weighted linear projections are applied in array operations. Only the final alpha-beta state update is sequential. This structure achieved **0.01 s runtime** and an **efficiency score of 1.00**, faster than the IRLS/Kalman fusion approaches in Generation 22 (**0.16 s**) and Generation 29 (**0.19 s**).

- **The best program’s regression design directly supports low-lag causal output.** It uses `t = arange(window_size) - (window_size - 1)`, making the regression intercept the level at the newest window endpoint, and precomputes a weighted least-squares projection matrix. The exponential weighting emphasizes recent samples while still stabilizing the local slope. This design supports its acceptable **0.39 lag error** and **0.72 responsiveness** without the extreme instability seen in more predictive filters.

- **Its gain adaptation is tied to local slope evidence rather than innovation size alone.** The tracker sets alpha from **0.42 to 0.72** according to `abs(local_slope) / (abs(local_slope) + local_noise)`. This lowers measurement responsiveness in locally flat/noisy regions and raises it when directional evidence is strong. Generation 19 instead relied on adaptive innovation noise in a Kalman-style update, which retained better lag (**0.29**) but much worse smoothness (**0.07**).

- **The current best uses a state-dependent output-level reversal test rather than merely a direction-label hysteresis test.** Its condition checks `step * velocity < 0` and rejects weak opposing proposed steps below `0.16 * local_noise + 0.08 * abs(velocity)`. This combines local volatility and established momentum in the same decision. The newer volatility-scaled consensus approach in Generation 30 also achieved **0.11 smoothness** and **125.6 false reversals**, but did not match the best program’s **81.2 false reversals**, indicating that the exact placement of suppression within the tracker update is consequential.

## Performance Analysis

- **None of Generations 19–30 exceeded the current best’s 0.55 combined score.** The closest results were Generations 23 and 25 at **0.54**, followed by Generations 22, 29, and 30 at **0.53**. The remaining variants clustered at **0.52**. This confirms that the current best’s comparatively conservative trend tracker remains the strongest evaluated trade-off.

- **The 0.54 Kalman-consensus programs surpassed the current best in correlation, noise reduction, and lag, but lost on stability.** Generation 25 achieved **0.93 correlation**, **0.57 noise reduction**, and **0.29 lag error**, compared with the best program’s **0.89**, **0.39**, and **0.39**. However, it had **128.2 false reversals** and **162.6 slope changes**, versus **81.2** and **102.6**, which corresponds to smoothness of only **0.11** rather than **0.16**.

- **Across the newer programs, high responsiveness generally coincided with poor directional stability.** Generations 22 and 29 had the highest responsiveness (**0.80**) and lowest lag/average error (**0.25**), but also the largest instability among the newer methods: around **247 slope changes**, **194 false reversals**, and **0.07 smoothness**. The current best’s lower responsiveness (**0.72**) is associated with much stronger suppression of short-term direction changes.

- **Runtime was not the differentiating factor among the leading designs.** The current best ran in **0.01 s**; Generations 23, 25, and 30 ran in **0.02 s**; and most other variants remained under **0.07 s**, all with efficiency **1.00**. The decisive metric pattern is smoothness and reversal behavior: the current best’s **0.16 smoothness** is meaningfully above the **0.07–0.11** range of every newly evaluated program.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Add a one-window pending-reversal state to the existing countertrend suppression rule.** Keep the current `step * velocity < 0` and volatility-scaled reversal band, but store the direction and magnitude of a suppressed opposing step; release only if the next local slope and candidate step agree with that direction and exceed a stronger threshold such as `0.25–0.35 * residual_noise`. This directly extends the mechanism responsible for the best false-reversal result while reducing the risk of holding the output flat through a genuine turning point.

2. **Compute residual MAD after the clipped weighted regression and use it separately from raw window MAD.** After estimating `measurements` and `local_slopes`, calculate fitted-window residuals and derive `residual_noise = 1.4826 * median(abs(residuals - median(residuals)))`; retain raw MAD for outlier clipping but use residual noise for `slope_evidence`, alpha scheduling, and reversal-band sizing. This distinguishes coherent ramps—which have high raw MAD but low fit residuals—from truly noisy windows, allowing faster response on clean trends without weakening robust local conditioning.

3. **Use hysteretic trend-confidence modes to stabilize gain adaptation rather than changing alpha continuously every sample.** Define a confidence ratio such as `abs(local_slope) / (abs(local_slope) + residual_noise)`, enter trend mode only after it exceeds a high threshold for two windows, and leave trend mode only after it stays below a lower threshold for multiple windows. In flat mode, use lower alpha and a wider countertrend band; in trend mode, retain the current higher alpha range and modestly increase velocity adaptation, targeting better responsiveness while avoiding the high slope-change counts of reactive Kalman-style designs.

4. **Precompute two or three endpoint-aligned weighted regressions and confidence-blend their measurements before the alpha-beta stage.** For example, retain the current `exp(-3, 0)` profile, add a gentler `exp(-1.5, 0)` profile for flat/noisy conditions, and a more recent `exp(-4.5 or -5, 0)` profile for sustained high-confidence trends; blend toward the recent profile only when slope confidence is high and residual noise is low. This preserves the proven causal endpoint alignment and vectorized implementation while offering a controlled way to reduce the current `0.39` lag error.

5. **Update velocity toward a bounded fusion of accepted output step and robust local slope.** Replace the velocity target based only on `step` with `target_velocity = w * step + (1 - w) * local_slope`, where `w` remains larger in noisy or flat conditions, then clip `target_velocity - velocity` to a residual-noise-scaled acceleration limit before smoothing it. The local slope already comes from the successful robust endpoint fit, so incorporating it can improve sustained-trend propagation while acceleration limiting should preserve the current filter’s unusually low directional jitter.
