# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **The current best Robust Adaptive Local-Trend Filter remains the strongest overall design at a 0.55 combined score.** Its pipeline—per-window median/MAD clipping, exponentially weighted endpoint local-linear regression, followed by a causal alpha-beta tracker—achieved the best directional stability observed: **102.6 slope changes**, **81.2 false reversals**, and a **0.16 smoothness score**. Its **0.89 correlation**, **0.72 responsiveness**, and **0.39 lag error** show that it accepts modestly lower raw tracking speed in exchange for much better output stability.

- **Causal endpoint regression is consistently effective for preserving trends without moving-average delay.** The current best evaluates its weighted regression at the newest point in each trailing window, rather than at the center or average location of the window. Related endpoint-regression programs also performed reliably: Generation 15’s **Robust Adaptive Endpoint Trend Filter** scored **0.52** with **0.90 correlation** and **0.75 responsiveness**, while Generation 21’s Huber endpoint-regression tracker scored **0.52** with **0.90 correlation** and **0.74 responsiveness**. This supports the value of endpoint-aligned local trend measurements for low-lag causal filtering.

- **Robust local preprocessing is associated with better reversal behavior than innovation-only robustness.** The current best clips each trailing window using local median ± `3.5 * 1.4826 * MAD`, yielding only **81.2 false reversals**. In contrast, the Kalman-oriented methods with clipped innovations and adaptive covariance estimates remained much less directionally stable: Generation 10 had **261/325 false reversals/slope changes**, Generation 13 had **303.8/375**, and Generation 14 had **197.6/249.2**. Robustly conditioning the local measurement before tracking appears more effective than reacting only after an innovation has occurred.

- **Explicit volatility-scaled reversal suppression materially improves smoothness.** The current best rejects a small step opposing current velocity when it falls within `0.16 * local_noise + 0.08 * abs(velocity)`. This direct state-dependent test coincides with its leading **0.16 smoothness score**, compared with **0.05–0.13** among the evaluated Kalman and regression-tracker variants. Generation 17, which also used reversal confirmation, was comparatively stable among newer variants at **101 false reversals** and **128.4 slope changes**, but still scored **0.51**, below the current best because it retained weaker smoothness (**0.13**) and lower overall balance.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **Highly reactive constant-velocity Kalman tuning consistently produced excessive short-term directional instability.** Generation 10’s Innovation-Gated Adaptive Kalman Trend Filter had strong raw tracking metrics—**0.92 correlation**, **0.84 responsiveness**, and **0.19 lag error**—but only a **0.52 combined score** because smoothness was **0.06**, with **261 false reversals** and **325 slope changes**. Generation 13 showed the same pattern more strongly: **0.12 lag error** and **0.89 correlation**, but **375 slope changes**, **303.8 false reversals**, and just **0.05 smoothness**.

- **Combining multiple predictive estimators did not solve the smoothness limitation.** Generation 14’s Adaptive Kalman Consensus Signal Filter was the strongest newer competitor at **0.54**, with **0.94 correlation**, **0.61 noise reduction**, and **0.27 lag error**. However, its consensus/post-tracking stage still generated **249.2 slope changes** and **197.6 false reversals**, producing only **0.07 smoothness**. Its superior noise reduction and correlation did not outweigh the current best program’s substantially better directional stability.

- **Short confirmation rules alone were weaker than direct suppression of small countertrend steps.** Generations 12, 18, and 21 all used two-sample or consecutive-opposing-direction hysteresis, but scored **0.52** and retained **142.8–188.6 slope changes** and **142.8–164 false reversals**. These mechanisms recognize reversal evidence after oscillatory behavior begins, whereas the current best’s step-level reversal band directly prevents weak opposing moves from changing the state.

- **Multiscale consensus added complexity without a composite-score advantage.** Generation 16’s Multiscale Robust Trend Consensus Filter used short- and long-horizon robust regressions plus a bounded-velocity tracker, but scored **0.51**. It maintained respectable **0.89 correlation**, **0.73 responsiveness**, and **0.38 lag error**, yet had **192 slope changes**, **158.6 false reversals**, and only **0.09 smoothness**. Its added cross-scale fusion did not improve the principal weakness shared by the reactive tracker designs.

## Implementation Insights

- **The current best separates measurement construction from temporal state tracking efficiently.** It first forms all trailing windows with `sliding_window_view`, computes local median/MAD statistics vectorially, clips outliers, and applies a precomputed weighted least-squares projection to obtain every endpoint measurement and slope. Only the final alpha-beta state update is sequential. This structure achieved **0.01 s execution time** and **1.00 efficiency**, matching the fastest programs while retaining robust local trend estimation.

- **The weighted design matrix specifically minimizes endpoint lag.** The time coordinate ends at zero for the newest sample, so the regression intercept is the fitted current level. The exponential weights `exp(linspace(-3, 0, window_size))` emphasize recent observations while retaining enough trailing history to stabilize the slope estimate. This implementation aligns with the best program’s moderate but reliable **0.72 responsiveness** and **0.39 lag error**, without the instability of the more predictive Kalman filters.

- **The current best adapts tracking gain using slope evidence, not innovation magnitude alone.** Its alpha varies from **0.42 to 0.72** based on `abs(local_slope) / (abs(local_slope) + local_noise)`. This gives larger updates when local directional movement exceeds local volatility and smaller updates near flat/noisy segments. By comparison, Generation 10’s innovation-gated Kalman logic achieved faster response (**0.84**) but was much less smooth (**0.06**) and substantially more reversal-prone.

- **The current best’s reversal logic operates on the proposed level step relative to existing momentum.** Rather than simply flipping velocity after two opposing samples, it tests whether `step * velocity < 0` and freezes weak countertrend steps inside a noise- and velocity-scaled band. This implementation is consistent with the largest observed stability advantage: **81.2 false reversals**, versus **101** for Generation 17, **145.6** for Generation 15, **164** for Generation 18, and **197.6** for Generation 14.

The current best’s runtime is tied for the fastest at **0.01 s**, but its decisive distinction is **0.16 smoothness** and **81.2 false reversals**. Generation 14’s stronger correlation (**0.94 vs. 0.89**) and noise reduction (**0.61 vs. 0.39**) were insufficient to overcome its **197.6 false reversals** and **0.07 smoothness**.

The current best’s exact combination of robust clipping, recency weighting, adaptive gain, and velocity-scaled step suppression remains the most successful observed balance.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Add evidence-gated release to the existing reversal band rather than replacing the band.** Keep the current `step * velocity < 0` suppression rule, but track one pending opposite-direction observation and permit a reversal only after two consecutive local slopes oppose `velocity` with magnitude above a threshold such as `0.3 * local_noise`. This extends the best program’s strongest feature—direct volatility-scaled suppression—while reducing the risk that genuine reversals remain frozen and contribute to the current `0.39` lag error.

2. **Use a robust residual-noise estimate from the weighted local regression to replace or augment raw MAD.** After the initial clipped endpoint fit, calculate residuals `clipped - (measurement[:, None] + slope[:, None] * t)` and derive a residual MAD per window; use this scale in `slope_evidence`, the reversal band, and gain scheduling. Raw window MAD rises naturally on legitimate slopes, whereas residual scale more directly measures unexplained noise, potentially allowing faster updates on clean ramps without sacrificing the current best false-reversal behavior.

3. **Introduce a two-state trend/flat controller with hysteresis for alpha and velocity updates.** Enter a trend state only when slope-to-residual-noise evidence exceeds a higher threshold for multiple windows, and return to flat state only after it remains below a lower threshold; use lower alpha and stronger suppression in flat state, then higher alpha and a slightly faster velocity update in trend state. This should avoid rapid gain fluctuations around noisy local-slope thresholds while improving responsiveness beyond `0.72` during sustained movement.

4. **Blend a small set of precomputed causal endpoint regressions according to local trend confidence.** Compute endpoint measurements and slopes using, for example, gentle, current, and steep exponential decays (`exp(linspace(-1.5,0,W))`, `exp(linspace(-3,0,W))`, and `exp(linspace(-5,0,W))`), then select or smoothly blend them using slope evidence and residual noise. Retain gentler weighting in flat/noisy windows for stability, but favor stronger recency weighting during coherent trends to reduce endpoint lag without adopting the unstable reactive behavior seen in Kalman variants.

5. **Update velocity toward a bounded consensus of tracker step and local fitted slope.** Replace `velocity = 0.72 * velocity + 0.28 * step` with a target such as `target_velocity = 0.5 * step + 0.5 * local_slopes[i]`, then limit `target_velocity - velocity` to a noise-scaled acceleration bound before applying the velocity smoothing. The local regression slope is already robustly estimated and contains directional information unavailable from the level step alone; bounding acceleration preserves the successful low-reversal tracker behavior while helping persistent trends propagate more quickly.
