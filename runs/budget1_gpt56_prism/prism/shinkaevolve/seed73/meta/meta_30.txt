# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- **Generation 19’s diversified construction plus lexicographic compound local search remains the best observed pattern.** The current best, **“lexicographic_bottleneck_search,” scored 26.23** (`max_kvpr` 25.23, 0.01 s, 100% success). It evaluates ten seeds: five model sort orders—pressure/size, size/pressure, pressure×size, pressure density, and size with inverse pressure—and two tie policies. Each placement decision minimizes the *projected global maximum* `load / remaining_memory` ratio, directly matching the KV-cache-pressure objective.

- **Strict lexicographic pressure-vector improvement gives a small but repeatable advantage over max-only comparisons.** Generation 19 compares the entire descending tuple of GPU pressures, allowing an accepted action to preserve the maximum while lowering a secondary bottleneck. This slightly exceeds close multi-start variants: Generation 29 scored **26.21**, Generation 20 and Generation 27 scored **26.20**, and Generation 21/28 scored **25.85**. The 0.02–0.38 score margin is small, but it consistently favors the current program’s full-vector criterion.

- **Compound exchange neighborhoods are associated with the highest-quality placements.** Generation 19 uses relocations and swaps first, then attempts one-for-two exchanges only if no ordinary improvement exists. This preserves speed while allowing the search to cross capacity-fragmentation barriers. The best score of **26.23** is above move/swap/exchange variants from Generation 23 (**25.83**), Generation 24 and 26 (**25.90**), and Generation 22/25 (**25.92**). Previous results also showed Generation 17’s one-for-two exchange version at **26.21**, reinforcing the value of compound exchanges near the top range.

- **Multi-start ordering is more valuable than a single memory-aware greedy order.** Generation 19’s ten construction paths outperform the single-sort memory-aware family: Generation 23’s pressure-and-size greedy approach reached **25.83**, while Generations 24 and 26 reached **25.90**. The diverse seeds address both pressure imbalance and bin-packing fragmentation before local search begins.

## Ineffective Approaches

- **More seeds or broader search alone did not exceed the current best.** Generation 20 used six orderings, two tie modes, and plateau-safe local search but scored **26.20** at 0.04 s, below Generation 19’s **26.23** at 0.01 s. Generation 29 similarly used seven orderings and two policies but scored **26.21**. The evidence indicates that Generation 19’s particular five-order seed portfolio and bottleneck-pruned refinement are at least as effective as expanding initialization diversity.

- **Max-focused or weaker local-search formulations underperform full lexicographic refinement.** Generation 27’s multi-seed bottleneck-focused moves, swaps, and one-for-two exchanges scored **26.20**, but it is described primarily around minimizing maximum pressure rather than Generation 19’s complete sorted pressure vector. Likewise, Generation 21/28’s greedy local-search variants with ejection chains reached only **25.85**. Reducing secondary pressure bottlenecks appears important for obtaining the final marginal improvement.

- **Complex exchange coverage without the current best’s staged acceptance strategy produced lower scores.** Generation 22 and 25 include broader two-for-one or one-for-two/two-for-one exchanges but scored **25.92**. Generation 26 included both directions of multi-model exchange and scored **25.90**. In contrast, Generation 19 delays its one-for-two neighborhood until moves and swaps find no lexicographic improvement, concentrating expensive structural changes specifically on local optima blocked by memory constraints.

- **The prior correctness lesson still applies: optimization is irrelevant if feasibility logic is unsafe.** Earlier Generation 4 scored **0.00** because unreachable swap/candidate code referenced undefined state. All high-scoring programs—including Generation 19 and Generations 20–29—explicitly preserve capacity during construction and every local-search mutation, and all recorded a **1.00 success rate**.

## Implementation Insights

- **Generation 19’s `PlacementState` makes the optimization both safe and cheap.** It maintains `groups`, pressure `loads`, and `remaining` memory together. Its `add`, `move`, `swap`, and `exchange` operations update each representation consistently. This state design explains the perfect validation result and supports rapid incremental scoring rather than reconstructing every placement after each candidate action.

- **The current best uses projected global pressure during greedy construction, not merely target-GPU load.** In `projected_add_score`, the program recalculates the candidate GPU’s post-add ratio and takes the maximum across all GPUs. It then selects `(projected_score, tie_memory, gpu)`. This is more objective-aligned than choosing a GPU from local load, local remaining memory, or a simple best-fit rule. Packing versus spreading is used only as a tie-breaker, retaining the pressure metric as the primary decision.

- **Bottleneck-pruned best-improvement search is the key runtime-control mechanism.** Generation 19 considers relocations only *out of GPUs currently at the maximum pressure*, and pair actions only if at least one endpoint is a bottleneck. Despite evaluating ten seeds and searching moves, swaps, and exchanges, it reports **0.01 s**. This contrasts favorably with Generation 20’s related six-order approach, which took **0.04 s** without a higher score.

- **Strict tuple comparison prevents cycles while permitting useful non-max improvements.** `key_after(...)` computes the full sorted pressure tuple after a hypothetical action, and candidate actions are accepted only when `candidate_key < best_key`. This differs from accepting arbitrary equal-score plateau actions: it permits progress on secondary bottlenecks but guarantees a strict global ordering improvement. The implementation therefore combines the stability of strict improvement with more sensitivity than maximum-only scoring.

## Performance Analysis

- **Generation 19 leads the supplied evaluated set, though by a narrow margin.** Its **26.23 combined score** is higher than Generation 29’s **26.21** by 0.02, Generations 20 and 27 at **26.20** by 0.03, and Generation 22/25 at **25.92** by 0.31. The lead confirms refinement within an already-successful heuristic family rather than a major algorithmic discontinuity.

- **The strongest alternatives cluster around multi-start, projected-pressure placement.** Generation 20’s multi-start KV cache placement and Generation 27’s multi-seed pressure placement both achieved **26.20**; Generation 29 reached **26.21**. This confirms that varied construction ordering, memory-feasible projected KVPR selection, and local exchanges are the dominant ingredients of the high-score band. Generation 19 adds the strongest observed combination of lexicographic action ranking and staged one-for-two exchange.

- **Single-initialization memory-aware methods form a lower performance tier.** Generation 23’s memory-aware greedy/local search achieved **25.83**, while Generations 24 and 26 reached **25.90**. Even though these methods perform moves, swaps, and some compound exchanges, they trail the current best by **0.33–0.40**. The measured difference supports the conclusion that multi-start seed quality materially affects final local-search outcomes.

- **Runtime does not trade off against quality among the leading designs.** Generation 19 is both the highest-scoring and among the fastest at **0.01 s**. Its score exceeds Generation 20 despite Generation 20 taking **0.04 s**, and it matches or nearly matches the effectively zero-time Generation 21/23/28 family while improving quality by **0.38–0.40**. The evaluation therefore supports bottleneck-focused, incrementally scored local search as an efficient implementation strategy.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Select the final multi-start result by the full sorted pressure vector, not only `candidate.score()`.** Store `candidate_key = tuple(sorted(ratio(load, remaining) ... , reverse=True))` after refinement and replace `if candidate_score < best_score` with lexicographic comparison of this vector. The local search already derives its marginal advantage from strict lexicographic pressure improvement, but the final seed selection currently discards that secondary-bottleneck information.
